# In 2022 We Sold Our Emergency Oil. In 2026 We Lent It. Different Companies Won.

Yesterday I showed you [how the government is lending out our emergency oil in 2026](https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/spr-exchange/), and I promised to compare it with the last big release, in 2022. I expected to compare two loans. That is not what I found.

In 2022 the government did not lend most of that oil. It sold it. That one difference changes who ended up with the barrels, and it changes what the country was left holding afterward.

## Same reserve, different winners

In 2022 the Energy Department held seven emergency sales and sold 180.66 million barrels to 16 companies. In 2026 it has lent 133.56 million barrels to 13 companies so far. The chart below shows what kinds of companies got the oil each time.

<iframe src="https://data4thepeople.github.io/spr-exchange/dist/compare.html?part=who" width="100%" height="1540" loading="lazy" style="border:0" title="Who got the oil: the 2022 sales and the 2026 loans"></iframe>

In 2022, refiners took 77.4% of the oil. Refiners are the companies that turn crude oil into gasoline and diesel. Trading houses, which buy and sell oil but do not refine it, took 5.5%.

In 2026 the picture is very different. Trading houses have taken 45.0%, and refiners 38.3%.

The largest buyer in 2022 was Valero, a refiner, with 25.7% of the oil. The largest borrower in 2026 is Trafigura, a trading house, also with 25.7%.

One caution. The company types are our labels, based on the name the government listed. A refiner can also trade oil, and a trading house can sell to a refinery here. The labels tell you who signed the deal. They do not tell you where any barrel ended up.

## Did the government steer the oil to refiners in 2022?

That was my first guess, and the records say no. Each sale notice says "the sale will be by price competition." Any qualified company could bid, and the highest prices won. I found nothing in the rules that favored refiners.

We can check this because, in 2022, the government published every offer from winners and losers. There were 583 offers from 20 companies. The table below groups them by type of company.

<iframe src="https://data4thepeople.github.io/spr-exchange/dist/compare.html?part=bids" width="100%" height="1010" loading="lazy" style="border:0" title="Who bid in the 2022 oil sales, and who won"></iframe>

Trading houses did show up. Five of them bid. But they bid low. On average their prices were $5.12 a barrel below the lowest winning price for the same oil at the same delivery point. Refiners' prices were 26 cents below it on average, and refiners won far more often.

The five trading houses did not all bid alike. One bid far below the winners, and another came within a dollar.

Trafigura did not file an offer in any of the seven sales. There is nothing wrong with that. A company bids when a deal makes sense for it. The question is why this year's deal made sense when 2022's did not.

## The two deals ask for different things

Here is the part I think matters most. The chart below puts the two deals side by side.

<iframe src="https://data4thepeople.github.io/spr-exchange/dist/compare.html?part=terms" width="100%" height="1700" loading="lazy" style="border:0" title="What the 2022 sales and the 2026 loans ask of a winner"></iframe>

In a 2022 sale, the winner paid the market price around the day it picked up the oil, plus or minus the small gap it had bid. The sale notice puts it this way: "The unit price for crude oil under the contract shall be the Delivery Reference Price (DRP) adjusted by the Price Adjustment Factor."

In plain words, a buyer could not lock in today's price and wait. Whatever oil cost at pickup, that is what it paid. All a bidder could compete on was a few dollars above or below the market. The bids show it: 96.7% of the oil was won at a bid within $5 of the government's reference price.

So what made a barrel worth more to one company than another? Being able to use it. A refinery with a pipeline to the reserve could put that oil to work right away.

A 2026 loan works differently. The borrower owes no dollars. It owes the same amount of oil later, plus extra barrels. As we saw yesterday, when oil for later delivery costs much less than oil today, a borrower can sell the oil now and arrange to buy back what it owes for less. Any company with a trading desk can try that. It does not need a refinery.

In my view, that is the key difference. A sale rewards the company that needs the oil. A loan rewards the company that can trade the gap between today's price and a later price.

Yesterday's post showed the same thing from another angle. In June, when that price gap nearly closed, companies took only 500,000 of the 40 million barrels offered.

I want to be careful here. This is my reading of the rules. The bids from 2022 fit it. They do not prove it.

## What the government could not control

There is a second explanation, and the data cannot rule it out.

In 2026, oil has sold for much more overseas than it has in the United States. That pulls barrels toward ships, and shipping oil around the world is what trading houses do. From March through July, crude oil exports from the Gulf Coast ran about 40% above the same months of 2022.

It was not that refiners sat idle. Gulf Coast refineries ran at 96.8% of capacity during the 2026 loans, compared with 95.0% during the 2022 sales. They also lost a supply line. From March through June, crude oil imports from the Persian Gulf were about half what they were in the same months of 2022.

And refiners were not pushed out of the 2026 loans. In the first round, companies took 45.22 million of the 86 million barrels offered. The rest went unclaimed. Valero and Motiva, two of the three largest buyers in 2022, do not appear in the 2026 awards at all. We cannot tell whether they bid, because in 2026 the government has not published the offers.

So both things are likely at work: the design of the deal, and the pull of higher prices abroad. I cannot tell you how much of the change belongs to each.

## What a loan turns into

Yesterday I said one risk of lending oil is getting it back on time. We now have the government's own records on that, from the last time it lent oil.

In late 2021 and early 2022, before the big sales, the Energy Department made 13 smaller oil loans totaling 24.42 million barrels. The department has posted the contracts, along with 30 later changes to them. We read all of them. Here is what they show.

- **Return dates moved on 10 of the 13 loans.** The department signed each change.
- **On seven loans, all or part of the oil was pushed back by two years or more.**
- **Trafigura borrowed 3 million barrels in January 2022, due back between June and September 2024.** A change signed on April 16, 2024 moved that to July through October 2026.
- **One change was signed on February 24, 2026.** It moved the return of the oil still owed on a 2.7-million-barrel loan to October and November 2026.
- **The extra oil owed did not grow with the wait.** Several of the changes say so directly: "There is no adjustment to the premium as a result of this extension." Others do not mention it.

It is important to be fair about this. The companies did not miss deadlines. The government agreed to every one of these changes, and three of them say the reason was a pause at the government's own storage site. The records also do not show whether the oil came back on the new dates. Only five of the loans have a final settlement on file.

But look at what this means for the reserve. Oil that was lent in 2022 and promised for 2024 was scheduled to come back in 2026, in the middle of the next emergency. A loan is a promise of oil, and the date on that promise has moved for most of the loans the government made last time.

## What a sale turns into

A sale has the opposite problem. The oil is gone for good, but the government gets money. In theory, it can use that money to buy oil back later.

So I followed the money. The chart below shows what happened to it.

<iframe src="https://data4thepeople.github.io/spr-exchange/dist/proceeds.html" width="100%" height="1090" loading="lazy" style="border:0" title="Where the money from the 2022 oil sales went"></iframe>

The Energy Department reports that the reserve's account received $16.99 billion from the 2022 emergency sales.

Then, in the spending law passed in December 2022, Congress rescinded $12.45 billion of it. To rescind money is to cancel the authority to spend it. That was 73.2% of what the sales brought in.

It happened in two parts of the same law. The larger part, $10.395 billion, sits in the same section that cancelled oil sales Congress had ordered for later years. The department counts those cancelled sales at 140 million barrels, so the reserve did keep oil it had been due to lose. The smaller part, $2.052 billion, is in the yearly energy spending bill. The records do not say what the rescinded money was used for after that.

That left about $4.55 billion. Nearly all of it went to buying oil. From 2023 through 2024 the department bought back about 59 million barrels, at an average of about $76 a barrel.

Put those numbers side by side. We sold 180.66 million barrels. We bought back about a third of that.

## My view

I went into this thinking a sale and a loan were two ways of doing the same thing. They are not.

A sale puts the oil in the hands of whoever needs it most that day, and it leaves the country with money. But money can be redirected, and most of it was. A loan promises that the oil comes back, with more on top. But the companies that find that deal most attractive are the ones best at trading a price gap, and the return dates can move.

In my view, neither design did the one thing I assumed the reserve was built to do, which is to make sure the oil is there the next time we need it.

There is one more difference, and it may be the simplest to fix. In 2022 the government published every bid, from winners and losers alike. That is how we know who showed up and who lost. In 2026 it publishes a single page for each round, with company names and barrel counts. We cannot see who bid, what they offered, or which return dates they chose.

Yesterday I renamed it the Speculative Petroleum Reserve. After reading the last round's contracts and following the last round's money, I am keeping the name.

## What this does not tell you

- **Where the oil went.** Crude oil is interchangeable once it enters the market. The records show who signed each deal, not where any barrel was refined or shipped.
- **Why any company did or did not bid.** We can see every 2022 offer. We cannot see the 2026 offers at all.
- **How much of the change is due to the design of the deal.** Prices abroad, the cause of the shortage and what refineries needed all differ between 2022 and 2026.
- **How each company should be labeled.** The company types are ours. Counted differently, the shares change, though trading houses' share is far higher in 2026 under every version we tried.
- **Whether the lent oil came back on the new dates.** The contract changes show what was scheduled. Most have no final settlement on file, and many of the amounts are blacked out.
- **Exactly how much oil was delivered or paid for.** Our sale and purchase figures are awards. The $16.99 billion received is the department's own count.
